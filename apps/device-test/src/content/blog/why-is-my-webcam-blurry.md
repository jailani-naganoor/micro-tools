A blurry or grainy webcam picture doesn't always mean the camera is poor. Often the real cause is lighting, a dirty lens, or the resolution a website or app has chosen. This guide explains the most common reasons a webcam looks low quality and what you can do about each one.

## 1. Clean the lens

Laptop webcams in particular pick up fingerprints from opening and closing the lid.

1. Gently wipe the lens with a dry, soft, lint-free cloth, such as a microfibre cloth like the ones used for glasses.
2. Avoid paper towels or rough fabric, which can scratch.
3. Don't spray liquid directly on the camera. If needed, lightly dampen the cloth with a cleaner suitable for screens.

A soft, hazy picture with glowing highlights is a typical sign of a smudged lens.

## 2. Improve your lighting

Poor lighting is one of the most common causes of a low-quality image. When there isn't enough light, a webcam compensates by boosting the signal, which adds grain (noise), and by keeping its shutter open longer, which can cause motion blur and a lower frame rate.

- **Face the light.** Sit facing a window or lamp, rather than with it behind you.
- **Avoid strong backlight.** A bright window behind you makes the camera darken the whole picture, leaving your face in shadow.
- **Use soft, even light.** A lamp bounced off a wall or a diffused light source looks better than a single harsh bulb.
- **Add more light** rather than turning up brightness settings, which mostly makes noise more visible.

## 3. Check the focus

Many webcams, especially those built into laptops, have fixed focus. They are designed to be sharp at a typical sitting distance, so sitting too close can make your face look soft.

- Sit back a little, at about arm's length from the camera.
- If your webcam has autofocus, it may hunt back and forth when there is little light or when something moves in the frame. Better lighting often helps. Logitech also suggests briefly covering and uncovering the lens to make autofocus try again.
- Some external webcams have a manual focus ring on the lens, or a focus setting in their companion software where you can turn autofocus off and set the focus yourself.
- Digital zoom crops the picture and enlarges it, so the image gets softer the more you zoom in.

## 4. Understand resolution in the browser

When a website or video app turns on your camera, it asks the browser for a certain resolution and frame rate, and the browser agrees on the closest settings the camera supports. This is sometimes called negotiation.

This has a few consequences:

- **The site may ask for a low resolution.** If a site doesn't request a specific size, browsers often fall back to a modest default such as 640×480 at 30 frames per second, even if your camera supports 1080p.
- **Resolution and frame rate trade off.** Some cameras can only deliver their highest resolution at a lower frame rate, or the other way round.
- **The camera's real limit applies.** No setting can make a 720p camera produce true 1080p detail.

You can see this for yourself with the [webcam test](/#webcam) on our home page. Choose a **Requested quality** (camera default, 720p, 1080p or 4K) and the test shows the resolution and frame rate the browser actually gets from your camera. If you ask for more than the camera or connection can deliver, you get the closest it can manage. The video stays on your device.

## 5. Video calls reduce quality to save bandwidth

In video call apps, what the other person sees is not the raw camera image. The app compresses the video and adjusts quality based on your internet connection, the other person's connection, and how your video is displayed on their screen.

If people tell you your video looks blurry but it looks fine in a local preview, the issue is likely bandwidth rather than the camera.

- Use a wired connection or move closer to your Wi-Fi router.
- Pause large downloads, cloud backups or streaming on the same network.
- Check the app's video settings. Some apps have an option for HD video that may need to be turned on.

## 6. Check the USB connection

External webcams send a lot of data. A weak connection can limit resolution or frame rate.

- **Plug directly into the computer** rather than through a hub, especially an unpowered hub shared with other devices.
- **Use a USB port that matches the camera.** Some higher-resolution webcams need a faster USB port to deliver their best modes. For example, Logitech says its Brio 4K needs a USB 3.0 port for 4K, and is limited to 1080p at 30 frames per second on USB 2.0. Check your camera's specifications.
- **Try a different cable or port** if the image freezes, flickers or drops to a low resolution.

## 7. Look at the camera settings

Your operating system or the camera maker's software may offer adjustments.

- **Windows 11:** Go to **Settings > Bluetooth & devices > Cameras** and select your camera to adjust its default settings, such as brightness and contrast. The options shown depend on your camera and your version of Windows.
- **macOS:** Built-in camera settings are limited, but many video apps have their own adjustments, and some external webcams come with their own software.
- **Manufacturer software:** Many external webcams have an app for focus, exposure, white balance and resolution.

Menu names may differ slightly by version.

## When it might be the camera

If you have cleaned the lens, improved the lighting and confirmed a good resolution, and the image is still soft, you may be reaching the limits of the camera itself. Built-in laptop cameras have to fit into thin screens, and older ones in particular can struggle in low light. An external webcam can be a worthwhile upgrade if you spend a lot of time on video calls.
